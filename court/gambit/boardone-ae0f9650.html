APPEAL ALLOWED

What you now know
  1. A tribunal is not a court. It is a few people at a table who decide one question. But it has to be fair in the same ways as a court.
  2. Nobody judges a case that they have a part in: not the person who made the decision, not a witness, not a relative, not a club-mate.
  3. Both sides are heard. The person who is accused must be told exactly what is said, and must see every paper, before answering.
  4. Whoever accuses must prove it. A suspicion is something that an honest person might do just as well, and “it is possible” is not “it is shown”.
  5. You ask your own witness open questions: what, why, when, how, where, who. That is examination-in-chief. A leading question carries its answer inside it.
  6. In cross-examination you may lead: a statement from your papers, with a hook on the end. “You could not see the screen, could you?”
  7. A record made at the time is worth more than a memory, because a memory grows. And a chess clock is two clocks: add up what has gone from both, and a score sheet tells the time.
