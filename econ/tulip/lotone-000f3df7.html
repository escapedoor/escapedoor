What you now know
  1. A price is what somebody paid; value is what a thing is worth to the person who will use it. When people buy mainly to sell on to a “greater fool”, the price can float far above the value. That is a bubble.
  2. A bubble needs new buyers with new money every week. When they run out, the price stops rising and falls back towards the value, but not to nothing.
  3. A futures contract is a promise to buy or sell later at a price agreed now. In a chain of promises every profit is paid by the last buyer, and if one buyer defaults, the loss runs back down the chain.
  4. The Dutch tulip mania of 1636–37 was real, and collapsed in February 1637, but it was smaller than Mackay’s stories of 1841: most promises were never paid in full, so few people were ruined, and the paper profits vanished.
  5. A true graph can mislead: an axis that does not start at zero, a short window that hides the long run, a log scale, a percentage from a tiny base.
  6. An externality is a cost or a benefit that falls on people outside a deal, like run-off into a shared drain. The fix is to bring it back into the deal: the polluter pays.
  7. A commons is used up when each user’s sensible choice adds up to ruin for everybody. Communities save it with rules, limits and watching each other, as Elinor Ostrom showed.
