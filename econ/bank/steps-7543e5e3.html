What you now know
  1. A bank keeps only a part of its deposits as cash (its reserves) and lends the rest. The money lent is spent and paid in again, so a bank’s deposits are many times the cash in its vault.
  2. A balance sheet: what a bank owns (loans, cash, its account at the central bank) equals what it owes (deposits) plus its capital, the cushion that takes any loss first.
  3. Solvent is not the same as liquid. A sound bank owns more than it owes, but it cannot turn loans into cash in one afternoon.
  4. Simple interest is paid on the first amount only; compound interest is paid on the interest too. Over many years compound interest is most of the money. The rule of 72 gives the years it takes to double.
  5. A bank pays savers less than it charges borrowers. The gap pays for the bank and its profit.
  6. A rumour can make itself true: each person’s sensible choice to queue adds up to the run everybody fears. Time, a lender of last resort and deposit insurance stop runs.
  7. Numbers mislead without their time, their base or their frame: £41 million “since 1923”, “up 50%” from two loans to three, a photograph of a queue for bread.
