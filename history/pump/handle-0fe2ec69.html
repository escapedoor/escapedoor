HANDLE OFF
Golden Square, Soho · 5 to 8 September 1854

What you now know
  1. A death register records when and where a person died, the cause as certified or reported, who told the registrar, and when it was registered. Read every column: deaths registered in a week are not the same as deaths that happened in it.
  2. To read a map of cases, mark each one where it began, then look at where the marks crowd and ask what stands in the middle, and what else stands there too.
  3. Water is fetched on foot, so “near” means near along the road, not as the crow flies. Where two walks are equal you can draw the line between two pumps.
  4. Two explanations that fit the same crowd of marks are told apart only where they disagree. The workhouse and the brewery, in the same foul air with water of their own, escaped; a widow on a healthy hill who drank the water did not. Cases that seem not to fit, once explained, make a theory strong.
  5. Ask what each writer could know. A housekeeper who was there every day is worth more than a newspaper that guessed.
  6. A fall that comes after an action does not by itself prove the action caused it. The attacks were falling before the handle came off, and most people had fled.
  7. To persuade a committee: say what you want, give the plainest fact first, answer the strongest objection before it is made, and say what it will cost.
Dr John Snow, the Revd Henry Whitehead, Mrs Susannah Eley and her niece, Mr Huggins of the Lion Brewery and the Board of Guardians of St James’s are real, and so are the pump, the workhouse, the brewery, the cap works, the workhouse’s 535 and 5, the brewery’s seventy and none, the numbers in Dr Snow’s table and the day the handle came off. The “one in five” is worked out from Dr Snow’s own sums. Tapping, Mr Peebles, Florrie Dawlish, Mr Stannard, Mr Mudge, Mr Pook and Mrs Applin are invented, and so are their words, the order of the days before the Board, and what was said in the board room. In real life Dr Snow did his own asking. ·
