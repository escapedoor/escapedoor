What you now know
  1. Writers make new words out of old ones. A compound joins two words (pepper + mint). A portmanteau squashes them together (breakfast + lunch = brunch); Lewis Carroll named it, and made ‘chortle’ from chuckle + snort.
  2. A prefix goes in front of a root and a suffix behind it: un- (not, or undo), -less (without), -let (small), -able (can be done), -er (a person or machine that does it), -ery (a place where it is done). To read a new word, take it to pieces.
  3. A simile says one thing is like another, with like or as. A metaphor says it is the other thing. Personification gives a thing human feelings or actions, and hyperbole is an exaggeration that nobody is meant to believe.
  4. A figure of speech is not true word for word. Ask what the two things have in common.
  5. Onomatopoeia is a word that sounds like its noise (snap, fizz). Alliteration is words close together that begin with the same sound: the sound, not the letter.
  6. A riddle has a literal reading and a figurative one, and the literal reading is often the trap.
  7. A cautionary tale, like Hilaire Belloc’s, warns you by making you laugh: the naughty one meets a fate that fits the fault exactly. That is poetic justice.
