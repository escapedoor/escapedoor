Every stave in its place

What you now know
  1. A Christmas Carol came out on 19 December 1843. Dickens called its five chapters staves, because a carol is a song and a song has verses; each stave has a job: the set-up and the warning, the past, the present, a future that may be, and the change.
  2. A writer can tell you what a person is like, or show you: by looks, by words, by actions, and by how other people behave towards them. Evidence is what is really on the page.
  3. A flashback goes back in time to explain the present. Change in a character shows in small things first: a tear, a wish, a short answer, a plea to stop.
  4. The order of telling is not the order of time. Stave Two’s past happened first; Stave Four’s future is a shadow of what may be, and it did not happen.
  5. In Dickens’s London 12 pence made a shilling and 20 shillings a pound. Bob Cratchit’s fifteen shillings a week was six days’ work at half-a-crown a day.
  6. After the Poor Law of 1834, poor people who asked for help were sent into Union workhouses, and debtors could be locked up. Dickens makes Scrooge’s own words about prisons and workhouses come back at him.
  7. The topic of a book is what it is about; its theme is what it says. You argue for a theme with evidence from the text.
If you liked this, read
  • Charles Dickens, A Christmas Carol (1843): the whole book is short enough to read in an evening or two, and much funnier than you expect.
  • Dickens’s next Christmas books, The Chimes (1844) and The Cricket on the Hearth (1845): you have already met their first pages.
  • Roald Dahl, Matilda (1988): Matilda reads Dickens in the village library.
