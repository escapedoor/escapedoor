What you now know
  1. A story is a sequence of events. Time words (soon, just then, once more), cause and effect, and what a character has and knows show the order, even when the pages are loose.
  2. A pun is a joke on a sound with two meanings: two words that sound the same (tale and tail, not and knot), or one word with two meanings (dry: not wet, and dull). A listener who takes the plain meaning has taken it literally.
  3. A parody copies the rhythm, the rhymes and the shape of a poem everybody knows, and turns its meaning upside down. Carroll’s crocodile copies Isaac Watts’s busy bee, the Hatter’s bat copies Jane Taylor’s star, and “You are old, Father William” copies a poem by Robert Southey.
  4. Nonsense is not the same as no sense. Carroll begins with something mad, and then follows it with strict logic.
  5. An argument turned round is a different argument: “I see what I eat” is not “I eat what I see”. One clue that fits somebody proves nothing; the answer is the one that every clue fits.
  6. A red herring is a clue that points the wrong way. An alibi is proof that somebody was somewhere else.
  7. “Lewis Carroll” was the pen name of Charles Lutwidge Dodgson, who taught mathematics at Oxford. He first told this story on a boat trip on 4 July 1862 to three sisters, Lorina, Alice and Edith Liddell, and the book came out in 1865, with pictures by John Tenniel.
