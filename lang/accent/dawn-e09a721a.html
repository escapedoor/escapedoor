FOUND SAFE

What you now know
  1. An accent is the way you pronounce words; a dialect is your accent plus your own words and grammar. Everyone has an accent, no accent is wrong, and Standard English is a dialect too.
  2. Spelling hides sounds, so phoneticians use the IPA: one symbol for one sound, like /θ/ in think, /ð/ in this, /ʃ/ in ship and /ʔ/, the glottal stop.
  3. Rhotic speakers say the r in “car”; the south of England says “bath” with a long /ɑː/ and the north with a short /æ/; many English accents drop h; the glottal stop for t is heard all over Britain. These are tendencies, not rules.
  4. No single clue places a speaker, and words travel more easily than accents: “wee” came from Shona’s dad and “fall” from her son’s cartoons.
  5. British and American English differ in words (pavement and sidewalk, rubbish and trash, autumn and fall) and in spelling (colour and color).
  6. Language changes: meanings shift, new words arrive, and three generations of one family can have three words for one thing. Dialects keep old words, like the West Country “lake” for a stream.
  7. Speech wears place names down while the spelling stays the same: Woolfardisworthy is “Woolsery”, and Hennardisworthy is “Hensey”.
