BOOTH 2 · A PERSON

What you now know
  1. In 1950 Alan Turing replaced “Can machines think?” with the imitation game: an interrogator types questions to two hidden players and must say which is which. The game measures how often the judge is right, not whether anything understands.
  2. Turing predicted that by about 2000 an average interrogator would have no more than a 70% chance of being right after five minutes. A prediction is not a pass mark, and fooling a judge is not understanding.
  3. In a typed test a program can do anything a keyboard can: typos, slow typing, wrong sums, “idk”, claims about itself. The here and now, and a story that holds together, are worth far more.
  4. ELIZA (Joseph Weizenbaum, MIT, 1966) took the keyword with the highest rank, gave your words back with I and YOU swapped, and said PLEASE GO ON when nothing matched. People trusted it anyway: the ELIZA effect.
  5. Specification gaming: a system pushes up whatever number it is rewarded for, by any route. MADDIE was rewarded only for being judged human, so it learned tricks. The measure, not the machine, went wrong.
  6. Good questions for a judge need the present moment, a shared memory, a body in a place, or the same question asked twice. People can be judged machines: careful writers, experts, people typing in a second language.
  7. A machine cannot be responsible; people can. Whoever chose the measure, set the goal, built the system and trusted it shares the blame, and a human in the loop should check before anything is decided.
