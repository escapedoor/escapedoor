What you now know
  1. Find the camera first. Two pairs of things that stand in line in a picture give two lines on a map, and the camera stood where they cross. What is in the middle shows which way it was pointing.
  2. A shadow points away from the sun. In Britain the sun is in the south at midday (about 1 pm in summer time), so morning shadows point west, midday shadows north and evening shadows east.
  3. A long shadow means a low sun, early or late in the day. In a real picture all the sun’s shadows agree. A shadow that goes its own way may have been made by a lamp, a flash or a window: find out what made it before you call the picture a fake.
  4. The weather in a picture can be set against the record for that place and hour: umbrellas, wet ground, breath, shadows or none. Leaves tell you the season, never the hour.
  5. A window, a puddle or sunglasses facing the camera show what is behind it, often the photographer, and writing in them reads backwards. A reflection that does not match its picture means two pictures have been put together.
  6. A crop is a choice. The same true picture, cut two ways, can tell two different stories, so always look at what is outside the frame.
  7. A caption says only what is known: who (counted), what they are doing (not what they feel), where, when (the day, not “last night”), and who took it.
