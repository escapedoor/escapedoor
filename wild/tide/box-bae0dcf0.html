HIGH AND DRY

What you now know
  1. A tide table gives the time and the height of every high water and low water. Heights are measured from chart datum, and sand that “dries 1.6” is under water whenever the tide is higher than 1.6 metres. So the dry time of a road is worked out from its lowest place: both ends, rounded the safe way.
  2. High waters are about 12 hours 25 minutes apart, so each day’s tides come about 50 minutes later. In a week, high water and low water change places.
  3. Spring tides, the biggest, come a day or two after new moon and full moon. Neap tides, the smallest, come a day or two after the half moons. Springs go further out and come further in.
  4. The rule of twelfths: in its six hours the tide rises 1, 2, 3, 3, 2 and 1 twelfths of its range. Half of the rise comes in the third and fourth hours, and on flat sand a small rise is a long way sideways.
  5. A tide fills the channels first, so the way back can be cut before the water reaches your feet. Nobody outruns a tide across a channel: go to the refuge, and stay in it.
  6. A tide table is a prediction. Low pressure and a wind blowing on to the shore make the sea higher, so it reaches every mark early.
  7. A fog signal is known by its pattern: how many blasts in a group, and how many seconds between groups. Count it against a watch, and the chart tells you which one you can hear.
