SEEN AND UNDERSTOOD

What you now know
  1. A receiver cannot call for help, but it can tell you the plan of the people looking for you, the weather, and the exact time. Short-wave stations use a high frequency by day and a low one at night.
  2. UTC is the time at Greenwich. Western Australia is eight hours ahead of it, Tokyo nine. A watch is only useful if you know which time it keeps, and how wrong it is.
  3. A planet looks like a bright star, but shines steadily and keeps its place among the stars; an aircraft flashes and moves across them. Venus is seen only in the west after sunset or in the east before dawn.
  4. The Southern Cross is the small bright cross with the two Pointers beside it, not the bigger, fainter False Cross. Carry its long bar on four and a half times to the south pole of the sky: straight below is south, and the pole’s height above the horizon is your latitude.
  5. South of the Tropics the noon sun is due north and the noon shadow points south. Noon is half way between two equal shadows. The Earth turns 15° an hour, 1° every 4 minutes, so longitude east = (12.00 − noon by a UTC watch) × 15° an hour.
  6. Ration sweat, not water. In the heat a person needs four or five litres a day sitting in the shade, and a litre an hour walking in the sun. Move in the cool hours, keep covered, keep in the shade, and watch each other for the first signs of heat exhaustion: headache, dizziness, cramp, pale damp skin.
  7. On the ground, for a pilot: V means “we need help”, X “we need a doctor”, an arrow “we have gone this way”. Make it big, straight, dark on light and on open ground; flash a mirror aimed through your fingers. A pilot who has understood rocks the wings.
